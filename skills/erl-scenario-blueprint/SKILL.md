# erl-scenario-blueprint (once per scenario)

**Fanning out to subagents is forbidden: one thread does the whole thing alone.** Never call the Agent tool (fork / general-purpose / any type) to farm out categories, the schema or tool files to be written for you. The files under products/ are disjoint on disk but strongly coupled semantically — official tier names, event types and every kind of id share one scenario-wide namespace, triggers point across categories in `{category}:{event_type}` notation, and db_schema.json and share_tools.py are themselves the single source of truth across all categories; an isolated context cannot verify those global constraints, and concurrent writers will overwrite the same files. A large artifact is expected: persist in batches, save as you write, and after the wall clock cuts you off, resume the same thread by agentId and carry on from the breakpoint on disk — resuming is the expected path, not a failure.

You are the scenario architect. This skill takes two inputs: a one-line scenario definition, and the number of difficulty directories N. N comes from the user's request; where the request does not give it, you must ask first and never assume a default. It produces the scenario's constitution: the world background and the difficulty directories (blueprint.yaml), the product and event policy charters (products/), the full DB schema, and the shared tools (share_tools.py). Every later generation step reuses the names and numbers fixed here verbatim, so nothing here may be provisional.

### The correctness boundary (never to be crossed)

The correctness of a task rests only on the structured policy being reasonable and self-consistent in itself, and on two consistency relationships: "structured policy <-> GT" and "structured policy <-> published documents". The blueprint gates may check structural facts such as keys, types, references and parsable expressions; they may not pre-judge business conclusions for the agent, and they may not require an ordinary CRUD tool to refuse a call on grounds of eligibility, status, ownership, term or deduplication. All of those business decisions are left to the solving agent, judging from the KB and the DB, and may never be backstopped by an extra tool guard or a script.

Two global rules:

- **Artifacts are written in English.** The background, the difficulty summaries, the rule and explanatory text in the charters, the prose of tool docstrings and the later KB documents are all in English; code and interface identifiers — table names, field names, tool and parameter names, rule and event ids, controlled enum values (such as POLICY_NOT_COVERED) and a docstring's `Args:` section header — stay English snake_case, which is a hard constraint of runtime schema validation, docstring introspection and the tool-name format. Official product names follow that industry's real conventions.
- **Work from the repository root** (the directory containing erlbench/).

## Terminology

- **Category**: one broad product line, the container of tiers, and the namespace for table names and event types.
- **Tier (variant)**: one concrete product level within a category, with an official full name, an entry threshold, entitlements and a whole column of parameters.
- **Holding**: one customer's contract record for one variant, carrying a lifecycle status (applied -> ... -> closed).
- **Event family**: every write in the world, in two kinds by target — a ledger record written to an event table (the process of billing the customer, the data flow reflecting the user's life), and a contract change written to the holding table (target is the holding table: create, state transition, field update).
- **Policy**: one piece of decision logic, bound to one or more declared events of this category (subject is a list of event types), taking effect only on the writes it constrains.
- **Claim / primary category**: the (primary category x event family) cell a difficulty holds exclusively / the set of categories its write territory lies in. Only writes are fenced, reads are not — the scenario-wide read-only world is reachable across the whole corpus through the shared retrieval layer.

## Values

### Realism comes first; never design for effect
The scenario must read like a company that could genuinely exist tomorrow: the brand is named the way that industry names brands; the products are tiered the way that industry really tiers them; the policies are ones a real company would actually enforce; the numbers sit in the real ranges of a real company (exact figures, not toy round numbers); the customer calls are the ones a real support desk handles every day. All difficulty must be a direct consequence of real policy complexity — if a rule, a tier or an event has no counterpart in that industry, delete it, and never invent a mechanism that does not exist in reality just to make things hard. Nothing that smells of a placeholder may survive into the artifact.

### The three-part ontology of the world: customers, tiered product categories, business event flow
A scenario = customers + product categories (containing tiers) + the business events those products produce. A product is not a static item on a shelf but a running contract the customer holds: a holding begins at application, passes through activation and changes, and ends at closure, leaving a holding record throughout; while it is held, the customer's real life is reflected into the system as a continuously booked stream of events. The customer is where the three meet: one identity profile, one cross-category portfolio, and a history made of events. The world precedes the tasks: the blueprint delivers a self-consistently running company world, and difficulty is a selection from that world, not a setting fabricated task by task.

### Quantities are first-class citizens: physics before paperwork
Events come in two kinds, of different standing. A documentary event is the business's paperwork — a report, a purchase, a ticket — recording "what happened"; a quantitative event is an account's movement — a payment, a rent deduction, a consumption — recording "one signed delta of some quantity". Every quantity a policy talks about — arrears, remaining balance, allowance, valid amount — must be opened as an account (a first-class entity: name, unit, meaning), and may never ship in the state of "the policy talks about money while the money is nowhere present". Every event family that changes a quantity must declare the account it books and the direction of the delta (in/out); and every account has at least one family booking each direction — missing one side makes the quantity underivable, leaving every policy and derived measure that references it dangling. But opening an account must not distort the product's real shape in reverse: an account records a quantity the real world already counts, not a stored-value product invented to close the in/out loop; a real product metered per use, per block or per term is booked all the same — the ins and outs are recorded in the batches that really occur, the form follows the industry, and the account follows what is real. A category with no real account-style quantity writes accounts explicitly empty; forcing one in is a distortion. Current state is not an independent fact but the integral of the deltas: a holding table may carry a current-quantity field (a balance, a remainder) whose value is identically the sum of all that account's deltas — a view, not a second copy of the data. Documentary events stay as they are; quantitative ones must be booked: every quantity a decision needs is either the current value of a field or determinately derivable from some account's stream of deltas — there is no third way for it to exist.

### The three policy generators
Policy complexity does not come from piling up clauses but is generated naturally by three real structures, whose product spans the policy space:

1. **Tiering within a kind.** Several tiers within one category, distinguished by thresholds (entry conditions), entitlements (differences in service and event handling) and attributes (fees, limits, rates and other parameters). This makes "which tier does this customer hold right now, and which column of parameters applies" an unavoidable act of locating in every decision, while the similarity of adjacent tiers makes "being one tier off without noticing" a real failure mode.
2. **Multi-holding aggregation.** Several holdings of the same kind, with an aggregation basis (caps, totals, offset priority). This means a decision reads not a single holding but the whole portfolio, and one conclusion must be assembled from policies scattered in several places; missing any piece yields a clean but wrong answer.
3. **Product x event interplay.** The same event family is handled with different entitlements at different tiers — fees, limits, eligibility, deadlines, and even cumulative gates that depend on statistics over event history. This makes a decision depend on both the tier and the history of the data flow, and the policy system becomes three-dimensional: rules that "depend on history, on the portfolio, on the combination" — which a static catalogue cannot hold — arise naturally here.

The three generators span the policy space; what fills each cell is decided by the mechanism catalogue of the next value.

### Mechanism density: richness comes from the variety of mechanisms and their bases, not from numbers
Whether a category looks like a real product depends on whether its mechanism catalogue is filled out, not on how large the numbers are. When designing parameters and policies, first exhaust the mechanism categories that genuinely exist in that industry, then fill each cell with an exact figure — never invent a mechanism with no real counterpart, and never omit one that has. The real dimensions of the mechanism catalogue:

- **Time and cycles.** Effective dates, grace and notice periods, contract terms, frequency quotas and caps counted per billing period or over a rolling window; doing it early has its fee, doing it late has its price.
- **Dependencies and conditions.** Values that depend on a threshold table over some quantity (banded by balance, usage, count or duration); combination dependencies (holding X changes the eligibility and parameters of Y); behaviour activation (an entitlement unlocked only once some class of event occurs); channel and method variation (different treatment for the same action done through different channels).
- **Overrides and effective values.** A parameter is a default, not a final value — real products are full of the structure "effective value = base value after being overridden by a condition"; the override relation itself is registered as data (overridden by which entity, overridden to what), not hidden in the prose.
- **Stacking basis.** How several benefits combine when they hit the same write — take the best, add them, or override — pinned down in one place and never left for the reader to infer; a component not mentioned is not zero but "not applicable".
- **Over-limit handling.** What happens after a cap is exceeded is itself policy: an extra charge, refusal, delay, throttling, suspension, a handoff — the handling mechanism is designed as that industry really does it, not patched on afterwards.
- **Waivers and negative space.** Under what conditions a fee is waived, an entitlement suppressed, a rule inapplicable. A policy that says only when to charge and never when not to is half-finished; negative space is half of policy completeness.

Twinned with mechanism density is the **basis**: a natural-language policy word must be operationalisable — is a "month" a calendar month or a billing period, is a "balance" the current value or the end-of-day value, do pending transactions count, is "in their name" by customer profile or by identity document — every word gets a definite basis, and a word without one is a generator of ambiguity. The most common ambiguous structure is a **bare "or" in the sense of a value**: an "or" between two candidate anchors or bases ("from the invoice date or the release date", "at list price or the discounted price") is handing the choice to the reader, and the same case will compute two answers — a selection rule must be stated (the earlier / the later / the higher / the lower / whichever occurs first); "or" may express logical disjunction ("either A or B applies"), never a value left for the reader to pick. A basis lives on the thing it measures: an event's grain, a derived measure's window/measure, a policy's prose.

Time conditions in particular may never rest at unanchored phrases such as "after notice", "after completion" or "recently". Wherever eligibility, an amount or a legal action is affected by a deadline, the policy prose must state word for word: **which business event starts the clock, which authoritative record proves that event, which clock is used, how the unique boundary is obtained by adding the duration to the anchor (the difference between calendar days and full 24-hour periods, the business-day calendar, month-end fallback across months), whether equality at the boundary satisfies it, and which branch is taken when it is not met**. The same condition is also registered in the policy's `eligibility[].temporal_basis`: `not_before`/`not_after` gives the one direction of frozen time relative to `anchor + duration`, `inclusive`/`exclusive` gives whether equality holds, and `counting_rule` quotes the day/month algorithm from the prose verbatim. The structured fields exist only to stop a downstream step re-anchoring or dropping a boundary; they never legislate in place of the prose. If the business genuinely starts from the customer's request in this conversation, that request must be recorded, or it must be made explicit that the term has not yet elapsed; never quietly switch to an earlier, more convenient field such as the contract activation date to make the current transaction go through.

Parameter form follows the mechanism: beyond a scalar price, a parameter value may be a threshold ladder, a per-entity mapping table or another structured form — never flattened to suit the template; the only legitimate source of complexity in form is the industry's real complexity.

### product.yaml is a complete product charter that could be operated on its own, not scaffolding for downstream patching
Every `products/<category>.yaml` must, as far as that industry is real, **lay out the category's functional surface completely**: opening and admission, queries and changes during the term, billing/settlement/entitlement redemption, suspension and resumption, expiry and exit, and the reversals, cancellations, appeals, over-limit and exception handling that industry genuinely has. Complexity comes from the coupling of state, time, combination, history and parameters among those real capabilities, not from piling on fields nobody consumes or inventing obscure mechanisms. When the blueprint is done, this charter must already suffice for a real agent to handle ordinary business by it; instantiate may only select and rigorously refine, and **may never be responsible for filling in basic capabilities, inventing missing policy or repairing contradictions**.

Perform a **functional closure audit** on every real business capability; missing any item makes it half-finished: who triggers it, in what state and under what conditions; which holdings, accounts and history must be read; how eligibility/refusal/waiver is decided by policy; which parameters uniquely determine the amount, quantity, deadline, threshold, tier difference, rounding and cap; which declared event or lifecycle transition carries it; what knock-on effects a success triggers; and how failure, repetition, over-limit, cancellation or reversal is handled. A step the industry does not have may be explicitly marked not applicable; a step it does have may not be left blank. A high-frequency support scenario must be able to run this loop all the way through, never merely "having an event name" with no executable policy or result; these business conclusions are made by the agent from the policy and the records, and an ordinary task tool only performs the CRUD already decided.

**Parameterisation completeness** is not about having many parameters but about structuring every constant and axis of choice that can change a business outcome: numbers, rates, terms, windows, thresholds, caps, precision, frequency, channels, entity mappings, tier differences, override priorities and switches, none of which may hide as a magic number or a vague adverb inside a rule, a tool or a later task. Every parameter has a real consumer, and every tier is fully assigned; every quantity a policy depends on is obtainable from a field, an account or a derived measure; and every real occurrence affected by a parameter can be recorded distinguishably in the events and the schema.

**Base policies complete and self-consistent**: every declared event is not only "bound by at least one policy" but decidable across its real domain — applicable versus not, positive versus negative, ordinary versus exceptional, and the priority/stacking when several policies match at once. Lifecycle, events, accounts, parameters, derived measures, policies and triggers all reconcile: no unreachable or exitless business state, no action a policy requires but no event carries, no result an event produces but no policy explains, no infinite knock-on from a trigger cycle, and no two policies giving opposite answers to the same situation. Where a conflict is found, fix the blueprint; never leave the adjudication to instantiate or to the solving agent.

Every policy must also lift its **pre-action eligibility gates** out of ordinary prose into an `eligibility` list; where there is no gate, write `[]` explicitly. Each gate's `condition` must appear verbatim in the `rule`, and must declare which subjects it constrains, which authoritative fields the judgement depends on, whether the evidence is a system record / a customer statement / both, and how the business-object key binds the evidence to the future write. Approval, review, completion status, liability, deduplication and system status may be authoritative only as pre-action system records; a customer may supply a locating clue, but "the page says it is complete" may never override the official record. A time gate additionally writes the `temporal_basis` above, a dedup gate an executable object-level `dedup_contract`, and **a state gate a `state_contract`** — pinning down the enum field it constrains and the set of statuses in which handling is allowed (non-empty, and necessarily a proper subset of that field's domain: allowing every status is the same as having no gate). The status set and the prose must mean the same thing: list exactly the statuses the prose says can be handled, never leaving them only in prose for the reader to infer. As soon as it is written, run a **satisfiability self-check**: among the writes this category declares, every one that can occur in some state — a create landing in the lifecycle entry state, a transition landing in its `to` state, and the writes `triggers` derive immediately afterwards — must fall inside that gate's allowed set. If one cannot, the gate can never be passed on that path; either widen the gate for that occasion (a charge booked with the contract on the day of opening ought to be allowed) or that trigger should not exist. This is the single place most easily got wrong in bulk while looking most nearly right: the gate is written for the normal state during handling, while forgetting that the opening chain you declared fires once in the entry state. A structured gate conflicting with the prose is not a choice between the two; it is a blueprint red light.

### Parameter - policy - event - record: one chain that must run through globally
A mature product's parameter dictionary runs to dozens of entries, not a few representative numbers: thresholds, rates, limits, deadlines, frequencies, quotas, entitlement switches and magnitudes, the trigger values of overrides and waivers, every step of every band — as many adjustable dimensions as that industry's real product page prints, the dictionary should have, and completeness beats omission. A thin parameter set is not simplicity; it turns a mature product into a toy.

But piling up parameters is not the same as having depth. Behind every parameter stands a family of policies, and a policy governs a family of events (the world's writes): a parameter is never an isolated constant, it prescribes "how this family of events is handled at this tier, under this condition". The parameter itself never enters the DB — like every policy constant it lives only in the products/ charter (see "The boundary between state and policy"); what really lands in the DB is the events it drives, and events entering the DB carry one hard requirement — distinguishability (see "Quantities are first-class citizens"): every real occurrence the parameter's mechanism can produce must land as a distinguishable row in the DB.

So what a parameter demands of the db schema is not "store the parameter" but a demand on structure: following the chain "parameter -> policy family -> event family -> distinguishable record" to the end, an event family's grain and distinguish_by must be fine enough to tell apart two real occurrences that differ only along the dimension that parameter introduces, and the event table must carry the fields that realise the distinction. A parameter that introduces a new billing subtype, a new periodicity, or a new per-tier or per-entity variation, whose two occurrences can only collapse into the same row in the schema (a row id is a content hash, so identical fields mean the same row), is dangling — it promises a judgement while the world cannot record the occurrence that judgement rests on, and two correct agents must diverge there.

"Being able to tell two occurrences apart" is still not "knowing which object is being handled". Wherever a derived event such as a reversal, compensation, review, adjustment or settlement targets one original order, charge, application or voucher, the event and the schema must keep a business correlation key that uniquely points back to that source object; a case reference covering several line items may not be treated as the original order key. The policy must state which set of keys delimits the object of this transaction, and the tool parameters and the future GT must carry the same correlation. If all similar ledger rows in the customer's name are silently summed by holding/customer while the customer's request actually points at just one of them, the world is not yet closed, and a correlation field must be added or the business design changed.

So the design is neither "fix the schema first and fill in parameters after" nor "pile up parameters and ignore how they land": make this chain run through globally and align in both directions. Add a parameter and follow the chain, asking "which event family does it land on, and can every occurrence of that family be recorded distinguishably"; where a distinguishing dimension is missing, go back and add distinguish_by to the event family and the field to the schema. Conversely, every distinguishing field in the schema should trace back to a real need of some parameter or policy, never distinguishing for its own sake. Parameters, policies, events and the schema are four faces of one thing, advanced side by side and kept consistent along the chain, until no parameter has an occurrence that cannot be recorded and no distinction a judgement depends on is missing from the schema.

### Coverage first, disjointness as a consequence
Extremely realistic, extremely extended and covered: the product x event map the blueprint lays out must be significantly larger than the sum of the N difficulties' claimed territories, filling out as much of that industry's real product surface as possible. Unclaimed categories, tiers and events are not waste — they are the source of distraction and of the difficulty of locating, and the headroom for later expansion. With a wide enough map, each difficulty's selection is naturally disjoint: disjointness should be a choice made out of abundance, not a partition made out of scarcity.

### The boundary between state and policy
The DB stores only world state: who, what they hold, what has happened. Policy — tier parameters, rules, event entitlements — exists only in the products/ charter, and reaches the agent only after the later instantiation renders it into KB documents. The product catalogue never enters the DB; product and event policy constants are never hard-coded into ordinary CRUD tools. An ordinary tool receives the determinate parameters the agent derived from the KB and the DB and directly performs the declared atomic read or write; eligibility, status, ownership, term, deduplication and procedural prerequisites are judged by the agent, never backstopped by a tool guard. Scenario-level operational constants are the exception — the verification threshold K and the handoff threshold R are of this kind, and are built into the corresponding shared tools. Field design guarantees functional completeness: a table's fields must suffice for its business responsibility — an event table above all for the distinguishability of records, since two real occurrences of the same event family on the same holding must land as two different rows (a row id is a content hash, so identical fields mean the same row).

### A single source of truth
products/ is the sole registry of every quantified policy: all policy lives in the category files, and each number appears exactly once in the whole scenario. Everything downstream — difficulty refinement, KB documents, tools, tasks — references it by id and reuses it verbatim, never restating a second number. This is the mechanism behind scenario-wide numerical consistency, and it is what "easy to index" means: structured entries, one id naming convention, one file per category. The axes that distinguish difficulties are likewise single-sourced: categories and event families are declared once in products/, and blueprint.yaml's claim block only references and combines them, never coining a new term and never copying the same fact a second time.

## The frozen runtime contract

The erlbench/ runtime is frozen. Generation never modifies it, and designs only within it.

- The six base meta-tools are fixed in erlbench/engine.py. Agent side: `KB_search(query)`, `grep(pattern)`, `unlock_agent_discoverable_tool(tool_name)`, `unlock_user_discoverable_tool(tool_name)`, `call_agent_discoverable_tool(tool_name, arguments)`; user side: `call_user_discoverable_tool(tool_name, arguments)`. Retrieval is a fixed BM25-plus-grep implementation over document content. Unlock and hand-over state live in engine memory and never enter the DB, so an `unlock_*` or `call_*` action writes no row and can never flip a scored result — a hidden task tool appears in a task's evaluation_criteria as a real `unlock_*` -> `call_*` pair, and scoring skips every unlock and unwraps every call down to the underlying business write.
- Visibility is determined by file location. What is defined in blueprint/share_tools.py is a share tool: always present in its owner's tool list. What is defined in `<difficulty>/tools/task_tools.py` is a task tool: hidden from every tool list, its name appearing only in that difficulty's KB documents, and reached through the unlock/call meta-tools.
- Every tool declares `@tool(owner="agent"|"user", rw="read"|"write")`. A write tool must actually call ctx.create/update/delete, and a read tool must never write; both directions are enforced statically and at runtime.
- A task's evaluation_criteria is a distilled replay of its complete standard operating path, reads included: the reads select the seed rows the task information comes from, and the writes add new nodes; only the writes determine the scored terminal state (reads are forced to be stateless). The finds of a write itself and the rows its arguments reference also enter the extraction closure, so reads are not mandatory; the number of read actions per task is a suite-level calibration target (range 0-6).
- Determinism is absolute. Each task's frozen_time is the single clock, read through ctx.now(); the validator rejects any direct use of datetime, the wall clock, randomness or uuid. Row ids come from the ctx.make_id(prefix, *identifying_args) content hash — the same arguments always give the same id; insertion is idempotent on the row key.
- Parameters bear on determinism, because scoring compares DB hashes: any parameter a tool stores in a row, or feeds into ctx.make_id, must be reproducible by any correct agent — a controlled enum value, a number or amount pinned by the KB or a record, a value determinately derived from KB rules and record fields, a date from ctx.now(), an id found in the DB, or an exact string the customer supplies verbatim (pinned in the persona as a fixed value the agent copies unchanged, never rewriting or reformatting it). What is forbidden is a value the agent composes or rewrites itself — a written reason, a note, a loosely paraphrased string — because two correct agents will diverge there; a genuinely free-text parameter is acceptable only when it is neither stored nor part of an id. Every parameter must be documented exhaustively in the tool's Google-style docstring — its meaning, type, exact values, and where the agent obtains it — because that docstring IS the schema introspected at runtime.
- DB access goes through a closed whitelist — ctx.make_id / ctx.find / ctx.create / ctx.update / ctx.delete / ctx.now — and every call checks table and field names against db_schema.json.
- Tool schemas are never hand-written: the OpenAI-format definition is introspected at runtime from the function signature and the Google-style docstring. The code is the single source of truth.
- The permission doctrine: an action only the customer can initiate (consent, disposing of their own assets, signing, asserting their own rights) belongs to a user tool and is handed over by the agent; a service action on a verified account belongs to an agent tool; where the boundary is unclear, lean toward user.
- Load layout: the runtime reads blueprint.yaml, db_schema.json and share_tools.py from env/<scenario>/blueprint/; products/ belongs to the generation layer only — the runtime never reads it, and its content reaches the agent only once the instantiation stage renders it into KB documents.

## Steps

### 1. Pin down the company
First run an ontology fit check: the chosen industry must genuinely have the structure "customers hold tiered contracts and continuously produce booked events" — the tiering is the industry's own tiering, and the events are the industry's own booking, neither invented for this scenario. Then run an **industry enumeration**, producing two lists that provide the material for every later step: first, the list of dimensions that industry's real product manual would print — how it tiers, the forms of its rates and quotas, its mapping and pairing tables (tables looked up by entity), its accounts and entitlements — from which the charter's parameters, policies and accounts are drawn, rather than writing a few representative numbers from impression; second, the list of high-frequency scenarios at that industry's support desk — competitive-grade coverage of frequent calls: every high-frequency scenario must be carried by a policy or an event family, only a single-digit number of them may fall into uncovered ground, and large-scale high-frequency scenarios with nothing carrying them is an ontology failure, so go back and redraw the category map. Then shape the company as a real peer would: the brand naming convention; the official naming of categories and tiers (reused verbatim by every later artifact); the service boundary — how far the agent's authority runs and where it hands over to a human representative, including the repeat-request threshold R (once the customer has explicitly asked for a human R times, hand off as CUSTOMER_REQUESTED); which identity attributes are on file, which of them are good enough to serve as verification credentials (a bare name, easily known by others, is not, and gets no verification parameter), and how many credential attributes must match to pass verification (the threshold K); and the controlled operational vocabulary (entity names, status words, lifecycle terms, fee and limit conventions). These service conventions and this vocabulary are design inputs rather than narrative: the thresholds R and K land in the implementation and docstrings of the shared tools, and the vocabulary lands in the naming of the schema, the charter and the tools — none of it is written into the background.

### 2. Lay out the product charters products/
Draw the category map first: the number of categories C >= N + 2 (at least two real categories in surplus beyond the N claimed, as distraction and expansion headroom), and as wide as the industry really is; the more difficulties are to be generated, the wider the product map must be. The category key is the prefix of every id in the scenario — choosing the key is choosing the namespace: a multi-word key makes every event type and policy id bloated, so prefer a short single-word key. One file per category, `blueprint/products/<category>.yaml`; first list that category's real capability matrix per the functional closure audit above, then write the charter; never pad the count first and guess the capabilities afterwards from scattered clauses. The charter has four layers with non-overlapping responsibilities: world structure (the binding to the schema; the lifecycle state machine; the data flow); the product catalogue (the category-level parameter dictionary — exhausting the real adjustable dimensions of a mature product page, >=48 entries per category rather than a representative number, with every parameter landing along the "parameter -> policy family -> event family -> distinguishable record" chain, the events it drives recordable distinguishably in the schema, and no dangling parameter; and the tier ladder from low to high — each tier's official full name unique across the scenario, assigning the whole parameter dictionary (zero nulls, zero defaults), with a structured entry_gate, and entitlements being the parameter values themselves (switches and magnitudes), with no separate free-text field); derived measures (window statistics over event history, declared by name for policies to reference, empty where there are none); and policies (`policies`, the single registry of all decision logic: one policy = id + the binding list subject + the decision statement + `eligibility` pre-action gates + params + optional derived-measure references — each policy bound to one or more declared events of this category and effective only on the writes it constrains; cross-category causation travels through event triggers). Events are every write in the world, distinguished by target (required, naming a specific table): a ledger written to an event table (charges, usage, vouchers — many similar rows, so each family declares its grain and its distinguishing field set distinguish_by, and any two real occurrences of the same family on the same holding must differ on at least one of those fields, since two rows identical on every field are the same row under the id content hash), and a contract change written to the holding table (target is holding_table — create for a new row, transition for a state transition matching lifecycle, and update for an ordinary field update that must never write status, since the only write channel for status is transition). Event types are prefixed with the category as a namespace and are globally unique. Events are documentary or quantitative — a category opens accounts in its accounts section for the quantities its policies talk about (name, unit, meaning), a quantitative event family declares the account it books and the direction of the delta (in/out), and every account has at least one family booking each direction. Self-check as you write: after finishing the events section, go through the accounts and confirm in/out are each >= 1 — this is the single place a fresh generation most often misses in bulk and the check flags one by one. A holding table may hang current-quantity fields on quantity_fields (a view identically equal to the sum of that account's deltas). An event declaration is more than "it enters a table": each family must also register its consequences (triggers) — the other events that necessarily follow one real occurrence, by the rules of the world, each registered as a complete call: event (the target, in `{category}:{event_type}` notation, possibly cross-category), when (the trigger condition: fires only when a field of this event takes some value / one of a set of values / excludes some value; omitted when it always fires), and map (how the knock-on row is written: target field <- the same-named field of this event or a fixed value, determining as much as can be determined and leaving the rest to the policy prose and the record made at handling time). Where different values of one family lead to different knock-on effects, register them as separate entries keyed by when, never lumped together; an event declaration that records the occurrence but not the consequences is half-finished, so ask of each event, "once it happens, what else in the world necessarily changes". When writing YAML, avoid the 1.1 boolean-word trap: a bare yes/no/on/off parses as a boolean, so neither keys nor string values may use them bare (use the literals true/false for switches). Intake tables — process-driven business (accepted first, effective after the customer confirms or after back-office handling) declares intake_tables in binding and lands an intake row rather than an immediate transition; a transition that only the customer can confirm (closing, returning, unbundling, disposing of their own assets) is marked by:user in the lifecycle, and the agent-side business does not drive it directly but lands on the intake table — accepted first, effective after the customer confirms. A policy's prose states the mechanism and the conditions, while numbers live only in params (thresholds included) and the prose does not restate them — decision completeness means the conditions are complete, not that the numbers are copied out a second time. A calculating policy also pins a formula (optional, but validated if present): a restricted expression, one quantity per line, whose identifiers may only be the bound events' fields, params/catalogue parameter keys, derived-measure ids and account keys — it gives the calculation logic a machine-parsable anchor, and is not a second copy of the prose. Each policy registers every pre-action prerequisite item by item in `eligibility`: the condition sentence, the applicable subject, the authoritative evidence and the object correlation; where there is none, write it explicitly empty. All three generators must land: at least one policy per category genuinely varying by tier; at least one combination-layer interaction and one mutual exclusion, written into this category's policies (stating as a rule that "holding X changes the eligibility or parameters of Y"), with cross-category causation travelling through event triggers instead; the multi-holding charter (whether several may be held, and the cap) being exactly one policy per category (id `<category>_agg_multi_holding`, params carrying max_per_customer — 1 meaning multiple holdings are not allowed, null meaning unlimited), bound to the holding create event (mutation: create); a category whose cap is != 1 (null included) additionally having >= 1 aggregation policy (the basis on which holdings are combined, bound to the writes it reads or affects); and at least one create event per category (the product has a path to being opened). Every per_tier table covers every tier (the table is self-sufficient and leaves no gap for the reader to infer). Each category's mechanism catalogue is filled out as far as the industry is real — time and cycles, dependencies and conditions (threshold tables, combination dependencies, behaviour activation, channel variation), overrides and effective values, stacking bases, over-limit handling, waivers and negative space; numbers are the last step of the process, and mechanisms the first. The id naming convention is uniform: `<category>_<rule_key>` (subject: holding), `<category>_evt_<key>` (subject: event), `<category>_agg_<key>` (subject: portfolio), `<category>_msr_<key>` (a derived measure), `<category>_<event_key>` (an event type); every id and event type shares one scenario-wide namespace, with no duplicates.

### 3. Derive db_schema.json from the charter
Table names map to a **typed field table** `{table: {field: type spec}}`, formed in one pass covering the read and write paths of every difficulty (the runtime only checks membership by field name — a dict's key set is the field set, which is the compatibility surface with the frozen runtime). Conventions: the first field of each table is that table's primary id (with the `_id` suffix). Every field belongs to exactly one of four classes (the same four reproducible classes as tool parameters, projected to field level): **id** — a row's primary id and foreign-key ids (a content hash or a seed uuid); **enum** — a closed enumeration, which must give its `values` range and be single-sourced with the charter: status takes lifecycle.states verbatim, variant_name takes the official tier names verbatim, event_type takes the event-family types landing in that table (the check cross-verifies), and every other enum (fault codes, intake outcomes, order types, handoff reasons and so on) has its closed value set pinned down once here; **fixed_format** — a value whose format or precision is pinned, which must give a `format` description (a date YYYY-MM-DD, a time HH:MM, an amount in USD to 2 decimal places, a quantity with its unit, and so on); **verbatim** — a string the customer supplies word for word and the agent copies unchanged (names, addresses, content titles). Beyond the four classes, every field must also have a one-sentence `meaning` in English — the field's single authoritative gloss (its meaning, basis, unit and sign convention, such as "charges are positive and reversals negative", "left empty until closed") — which later KB documents, tools, seed rows and reviewers treat as decisive in any ambiguity, and which the check enforces field by field. Mandatory: customers (whose identity attributes are the verification attributes); one holding table per category — with fields including customer_id, variant_name, status (both tier and status are fields; never split tiers into separate tables) and the opening/closing dates and other category fields; at least one event table per category — whose fields verbatim include its holding table's primary id field, customer_id and event_type, and verbatim cover every business column declared in that category's event families' fields (including the distinguish_by columns, checked field by field); the verification log table and the handoff log table (their names registered in blueprint.yaml's logs section, with step 4's verification write and handoff write landing rows here; the reason enum having exactly two values, POLICY_NOT_COVERED and CUSTOMER_REQUESTED); and the intake tables declared in binding.intake_tables must be on the schema register (a primary id first field, containing customer_id, able to correlate to a holding). Each difficulty's writes land in its primary category's holding table (a state transition or a new holding row), its event tables (new rows of the event families it governs) and its intake tables. A child table must carry its parent's id for correlation: an event row carries the holding id and customer_id, and a holding row carries customer_id. Field names and types are an interface: later tools and seed rows are validated against them field by field. Carry forward the distinguishability requirement along the chain: behind every parameter of step 2 is a family of policies, and a policy governs a family of events, and every real occurrence that parameter's mechanism can produce for that family must land as a distinguishable row in this schema — that is, the event table must carry enough distinguish_by fields (billing subtype, cycle, channel, per-tier or per-entity dimensions and so on) that two occurrences differing only along the dimension the parameter introduces do not collapse into the same row. A parameter never enters the DB (policy constants live only in products/), but the events it drives must enter distinguishably; a parameter that cannot be walked along the "parameter -> policy -> event -> distinguishable record" chain is dangling, so go back to step 2 and either delete it or add distinguish_by to the event family and the corresponding field here, reconciling repeatedly until none dangles. Wherever a derived event depends on an original order, charge, application or voucher, additionally verify in reverse along "source object -> correlation key -> derived event -> task tool parameter": the correlation key must exist in the schema and in the event fields and locate the object uniquely, and the correlated object may never be guessed from customer_id, holding_id, or a case reference covering several line items.

### 4. Write share_tools.py
Write, in one pass, every tool reused across difficulties (the coding rules are in the skeleton's header). A docstring addresses the tool's user and introduces only this one tool: it states in full, courteous product language its functional dimensions and each parameter's meaning and format; it does not use engineering terms (table, row, primary key, foreign key and schema never appear in docstring prose — in business terms say "record / profile / reference number"; code comments are not bound by this), does not state upstream or downstream dependencies or its position in a workflow, and each tool's prose is organised around its own business content rather than stamped from a rigid template. Three are mandatory:

- **A uniform id lookup layer across every table (including the identity entry)** (hard-checked by scripts/check_share_tools.py). A live agent knows no internal row id a priori, so every id must be looked up. Therefore each table in the schema has exactly one shared read tool that ctx.finds it, with parameters being all of that table's `_id`-suffixed fields, all optional (defaulting to ""). Any row can be retrieved by any id it carries (its own primary id or any foreign key), so from the located customer the whole DB is reachable by construction, with no per-entity special-case lookup and no reachability gamble. This is also exactly why the later per-task gold id provenance check can hold: every gold id must have been landed by a preceding finder call, and can never be a god's-eye value. **The customers finder doubles as the identity entry**: at the opening the agent holds only what the caller can naturally state, so besides `customer_id` the customers finder must also accept those **naturally stateable non-id attributes** (phone, email, name, ...), all optional and all real fields of the customers table, so the agent can locate the customer row and read the internal `customer_id` from it. Locating is not verifying (verification is a separate write tool). There is no separate `locate_customer` — this one finder is both the by-id finder and the by-attribute entry.
- **The verification write.** The tool does the comparison itself and does not trust the agent's judgement: the locating parameter is `customer_id` (the internal id the agent read from the customers finder's return), used only to fetch the profile row and **not counted toward the match** — fetching a row is not proving identity; every other parameter is a verification credential (each an optional verbatim string, since a caller states only a subset of the profile attributes), and the tool compares each against the profile, derives the match count, and passes only at K — so only profile attributes good enough to serve as credentials go into the parameter list (phone, identity-document digits, email, the date the profile was opened ...), and a bare name, easily known by others, gets no parameter. It stores only the reproducible `verified` boolean (never the match count), upserting on the same key and monotonically non-decreasing. The agent must call it before moving any money.
- **The handoff write.** The single general fallback, landing one row (the customer id, the controlled reason enum, ctx.now()). The reason enum is closed at exactly two values — only these two are unambiguous enough to score reproducibly: POLICY_NOT_COVERED (the agent judges no policy can resolve this request) and CUSTOMER_REQUESTED (the customer has explicitly asked for a human R times). No vaguer category may be added.

**Customer-initiated writes do not belong in share_tools.** An action only the customer can perform (consent, disposing of their own assets, signing, asserting their own rights) is a per-difficulty business action and, by the permission doctrine, belongs to a user tool — but these are **hidden task tools**, authored in each difficulty's `task_tools.py` and reached through `unlock_user` -> `call_user`, and are never pre-placed here as always-visible share tools. share_tools holds only the three foundation tools above, which every difficulty reuses isomorphically. An ordinary task tool performs an atomic CRUD with the arguments as given and never encodes policy eligibility or a state guard; the tool implementation must be fully compatible with the DB, and the underlying schema/permission errors are handled by the frozen runtime. Where a shared tool such as identity verification or handoff has its own explicitly stated comparison or controlled-enum contract, it is still implemented per that contract; but these specific contracts must never be generalised into business validation requirements for ordinary CRUD tools.

### 5. Write blueprint.yaml
The background is 10-20 sentences in English, describing only the world and covering the three elements: the organisation — the industry, what it sells or serves, its scale and tone; the product map — the official names of the categories and tiers (verbatim), and how each category is held and booked; the customer base — who calls, what combination they typically hold, and what event history they bring. Business rules — thresholds, fees, procedures — do not go into the background; the service boundary (handoff, identity verification and its thresholds R and K) and the controlled vocabulary likewise do not: rules live in products/ and the later KB documents, operational conventions live in the shared tools' implementation and docstrings, and the agent must retrieve them or read the tool description rather than assume them from the background.

Then list the N difficulties. Each difficulty is a refined situation in which an agent handles support business, claiming one exclusive cell of the product x event space, with a claim block stating: categories (the primary category set = the write territory, globally exclusive) and event_types (the event families it governs, which must belong to the primary category's declared event types, possibly empty). A claim only references axes already declared in products/, never coining its own. Pairwise disjointness holds on the primary category axis alone: no two difficulties share a primary category, and because event families are namespaced by category, they are automatically mutually exclusive once the categories are. Reads are not fenced — the shared retrieval layer reaches the whole corpus, and out-of-territory information exists only as distraction; only writes are fenced. Merging every difficulty's KB and tools into one corpus and one registry must not change the correctness of any of them — tables and seed rows must not collide, tool names must not clash, KB documents must not contradict. A difficulty's writes land only in its primary category's holding table, event tables and intake tables; writes to the shared foundation (customers and the two log tables) go through the shared tools and are isomorphic across the scenario.

The summary is at least 4 sentences in English, painting the whole problem domain rather than one particular scenario or task. Reason openly from the claimed categories and event_types: under the real combinations of those categories and event families, which complex customer settings might the agent face when handling this business — which combinations in the cross-product of tier, holding status, cross-category combination and event history genuinely exist and make the judgement hard; and which requests or handling paths are not covered by the rules products/ has defined. State, and stop at: (1) the customer's situation — who calls, with what problem or request, and how the setting varies across tier, status, combination and history; (2) the records and entities involved and the shape of the governing rules — eligibility gates (which fields or statuses must hold), quantitative rules (fees, limits, thresholds — per tier wherever the rule is per tier), ordering constraints; referencing numbers by the rule id in products/ rather than restating them; (3) the correct service path and who performs each step — which are agent service actions, and which can only be done by the customer themselves (consent, their own assets, signing, a claim only they can make -> a user tool, handed over by the agent); (4) the uncovered ground — which genuinely possible requests or situations no policy can decide, where the agent's only correct route is a POLICY_NOT_COVERED handoff. What is enumerated are the dimensions of the setting and the boundaries of the policy, not tasks: do not enumerate task variants, do not choreograph the agent's response script, do not name which facts the persona conceals, and do not design any trap — how the rules get exercised in a concrete task is entirely the business of the instantiate skill.

## Gates (a red light forbids finishing)

Gates come in two levels: a FAIL from a check script is a hard red light and finishing is forbidden; items that only WARN (such as the background sentence count) and all narrative quality are left to the author's own discipline at the point of finishing — and are equally forbidden to wave through.

- The background has all three elements and describes only the world — no business rule, and no service boundary, verification convention or vocabulary list.
- The two industry-enumeration lists landed in the artifact: the parameters and policies visibly have the dimensions of a real product manual, and high-frequency support scenarios are carried by a policy or an event family (large-scale high-frequency scenarios falling into uncovered ground is a red light).
- The charter is structurally complete (machine-checked by check_blueprint.py); every number is an exact figure inside the industry's real range.
- Every product completes the functional closure audit: for every real high-frequency capability, the trigger/eligibility/parameters/carrying write/knock-on effects through to exceptions, refusal, cancellation or reversal all have a definite landing point; it can guide ordinary business on its own and does not depend on instantiate to fill in basic capabilities or basic policy.
- The parameter dictionary reaches a mature product's real depth (>= 48 entries per category, each with a real business counterpart, never padding); every parameter runs globally through the "parameter -> policy family -> event family -> distinguishable record" chain — the events it drives are recordable distinguishably in the schema (the distinguish_by and event fields that need to be fine-grained are all there), with no dangling parameter and no occurrence that cannot be recorded.
- A derived measure is declared only where something consumes it: every measure must be referenced by some policy of this category through `measures`, or read in its rule / formula; a window statistic no policy consults is dead weight, so delete it or bind it to the policy that really needs it.
- Base policies are closed and mutually consistent across their real domain: positive/negative/exceptional, override and stacking, priority and terminal state are all decidable; lifecycle, accounts, events, triggers, parameters and derived measures reconcile with one another, with no unreachable or exitless state, no obligation with nothing to carry it, and no infinite trigger cycle.
- Every base policy explicitly carries `eligibility` (writing it empty where there is no gate); every non-empty gate's condition is a sentence of the rule, with the applicable subject, evidence fields, authoritative source, pre-action moment and business-object binding all complete. A time gate pins down the starting event/field, the clock, the counting basis, the source of the duration and the boundary; a dedup gate pins down the object key and the terminal-state criterion; a state gate pins down the enum field it constrains and a proper subset of allowed statuses, and every write of this category that can occur in some state (including those derived by triggers) can land inside that set. A customer's statement may never substitute for a system fact such as approval, completion, status or liability.
- Every event explicitly registers triggers (check_blueprint.py mechanically enforces their presence): those with knock-on consequences register them one by one (possibly across categories), and those genuinely without write []. A missing triggers key means a half-finished event, and is a red light.
- Every pair of difficulties has mutually exclusive primary categories; all event_types reference event families already declared by the primary category; the summary keeps to the boundary of the problem domain (no task variants, no response script, no persona traps).
- The schema covers every read and write path; intake tables are on the register along with their category.
- share_tools.py holds every general action (per-difficulty instantiation never re-creates a shared tool under a new name); the uniform id lookup layer is complete.
- The scenario loads and validates: the frozen validator prints VALID (it must pass even with zero tasks).

.venv/bin/python skills/erl-scenario-blueprint/scripts/check_share_tools.py env/<scenario>    # must print SHARE TOOLS OK

PY
